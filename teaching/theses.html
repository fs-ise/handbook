First supervision meeting

Use the first meeting to establish a shared, realistic project frame. Record the decisions in the existing internal thesis notes; do not create a separate tracking system.

Avoid extending the exposé phase unnecessarily. Once it provides a viable research question, scope, and approach, tell the student clearly that it is complete and that thesis work should proceed.

Advising and iterative feedback

Treat supervision as an iterative research-development process: the student develops and justifies the work, the supervisor challenges assumptions and gives directional feedback, and the student decides how to implement or discuss that feedback. Supervision does not transfer ownership of the research to the supervisor.

For each substantial feedback round:

  1. Prepare: Ask the student to identify the maturity of the material and the specific questions on which feedback is needed. Link to the relevant student-facing guidance rather than repeating writing or method instructions.
  2. Recommend: Give prioritized, actionable recommendations and distinguish required corrections from optional suggestions or possible extensions.
  3. Agree: Record the important recommendations and agreed actions in the existing thesis notes or meeting notes.
  4. Follow up: At the next exchange, ask the student to show where each agreed action was implemented or explain why it should be discussed further.

Throughout the project:

  • State expectations, changes in direction, and concerns about progress or quality promptly and transparently.
  • Relate feedback to the published assessment criteria; students should not be surprised by the basis of their grade.
  • Encourage students to seek feedback before uncertainty becomes a major project risk. Make clear that disclosing a difficulty early is part of responsible research practice.
  • Keep feedback proportionate to the stage of the work: emphasize direction and method early, then argument, evidence, coherence, and presentation as the thesis matures.
  • Begin check-ins with an open, respectful question about progress or current obstacles, while allowing students to choose how much personal context they share.
NoteConversation starters

When checking in with thesis students, begin with questions that express interest in their experience—without being too personal. Let students choose how much they wish to share. Examples include:

  • How was the start of your semester?
  • Is this a busy time for you? How many exams are on your plate, and which ones?
  • How are you feeling about your thesis these days? Any areas you’d like to focus on?

These questions help foster a supportive, respectful environment for supervision.

Publication and reuse opportunities

When a thesis makes a promising conceptual, empirical, methodological, or technical contribution, discuss whether it could be developed into a publication, research software, or an open-source contribution.
